## Purpose

To inform a decision on whether a flat $100 fee, unchanged since 2015, should be indexed to inflation, and to which index.

## Bottom line

By 2025, a $100 fee set in 2015 was worth about $76 in 2015 dollars, because BC consumer prices rose 31.9% over the decade [S1]. Indexing it to the BC or Canada consumer price index would have kept its real value; the two indices differ by about $2 over ten years. The main choice is whether to index at all, and whether to cap annual increases.

## Key facts

- BC All-items CPI rose 31.9% from 2015 to 2025; Canada's rose 29.7% (annual averages) [S1].
- Unindexed, the $100 fee was worth $75.84 in 2015 dollars in 2025, using BC CPI [S1, team calculation].
- Indexed annually, the fee would have been $131.86 in 2025 using BC CPI, or $129.70 using Canada CPI [S1, team calculation].
- Annual inflation was highest in 2022: 6.9% in BC and 6.8% in Canada [S1, computed from index values].

## Discussion

**Erosion.** Most of the loss happened from 2021 to 2023, when annual inflation was well above the rates of the late 2010s. A fee that stays flat loses real value every year prices rise, and the loss compounds.

**Which index.** The BC and Canada indices moved closely over the decade. BC annual inflation was slightly higher than Canada's in each year from 2016 to 2020, lower in 2021, and within 0.2 percentage points from 2022 to 2025 [S1, computed from index values]. The BC index matches where the fee is paid; the Canada index is the more familiar headline figure. Either is defensible.

**Options.**

1. **Keep the fee flat.** Simple and predictable for payers, but its real value keeps falling.
2. **Index annually.** Keeps real value, with about a one-year lag because annual averages are published only after the December CPI. Increases are small in normal years but can be large in high-inflation years.
3. **Index annually with a cap.** Protects payers in high-inflation years; the program absorbs some erosion.
4. **Review periodically.** Fewer changes, but larger jumps and a decision needed each time.

**Limits.** The CPI measures consumer prices, not the cost of delivering a specific service. If the fee is meant to recover costs, a cost index for that service may fit better. Annual changes here are computed from index values rounded to one decimal and may differ slightly from rates Statistics Canada publishes. Legal authority to change the fee is out of scope.

## Implications or next steps

If indexing is pursued, the next questions are the cap (if any), the timing of annual adjustments, rounding of the fee to a convenient amount, and how changes are communicated to payers.
